## Identification
Armyworms are caterpillars that feed in groups or alone on maize leaves and in the whorl. The model class 'Army worm' comes from a dataset of a different armyworm species, but in Nigeria the armyworm farmers most often meet on maize is the fall armyworm, so this entry covers both. To recognise fall armyworm: a pale inverted 'Y' on the head, and four dark spots arranged in a square near the tail end. Caterpillars hide deep in the whorl during the day. Egg masses are laid on leaves, covered with a greyish fuzz. Do not confuse with stem borers, which tunnel into the stem.


## Damage and symptoms
Ragged holes and windowing on young leaves, then large areas of leaf eaten. Fresh sawdust-like droppings (frass) inside the whorl. Damaged whorl leaves open up with rows of holes. Severe attacks leave only ribs and stems, and caterpillars may feed on tassels and cobs. Attacks on young plants hurt yield the most.


## Conditions that favour it
Maize is the preferred host, but the pest feeds on many other crops and grasses. Populations tend to build up later in the season, so late-planted fields are hit harder. Staggered planting gives the pest a continuous food supply. Weedy grasses around the plot also help it survive.


## Cultural and prevention practices
Plant early with the first rains and keep planting dates within a village close together. Use varieties with some tolerance if available, and keep plants well fertilised so they can compensate for damage. Keep the plot and surroundings free of weedy grasses. Intercrop maize where suitable. Scout early: from emergence until about shoulder height, walk a 'W' pattern across the field, stopping at five places and checking 10 plants at each, early in the morning when caterpillars are easier to find. Hand-pick and destroy egg masses and small caterpillars when only a few plants are affected. Guidance prepared for African smallholders suggests acting when roughly 20% of plants are damaged in the first two and a half weeks of growth, and roughly 40% from about three weeks until tasselling. Once plants are much taller there is little that can be done to save the crop, which is why early scouting matters.


## Biological and low-risk controls
Natural enemies such as parasitic wasps, predatory bugs, ants, and birds reduce fall armyworm numbers; avoid broad-spectrum sprays that kill them. Where a spray is needed, lower-risk options such as registered biopesticides are preferred over highly hazardous products. Ask the extension service which are registered.


## Chemical control (registered products only, label rates, protective equipment)
Spray only if scouting shows the problem is spreading. Use only products registered in Nigeria (NAFDAC) for this crop and pest, bought from a licensed dealer in the original labelled container. Follow the label for rate, timing, and the waiting period before harvest. Wear protective equipment (gloves, long sleeves, boots, mask, eye cover), never spray against the wind or near water, and keep children and animals away. Ask the extension officer which products are currently approved. The caterpillars hide in the whorl, so the timing and placement of the spray matter; early morning or evening, directed into the whorl, as the label allows. Choose products by their hazard class, and prefer lower-hazard ones.


## When to contact an extension officer
Contact an extension officer early if more than a small share of plants are damaged, if you find caterpillars on the cobs, or if you want help choosing an approved, lower-risk product. Fall armyworm is a national concern, and extension offices may have local scouting groups and alerts.
