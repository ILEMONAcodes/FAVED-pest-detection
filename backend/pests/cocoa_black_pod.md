## Identification
Black pod is a fungus-like (Phytophthora) disease that turns pods dark brown to black. It starts as a small brown spot that can appear anywhere on the pod and spreads quickly. In wet weather a white, powdery growth may appear on the blackened area. Two species cause it in West Africa, and one of them spreads faster and does more damage. Do not confuse with mirid damage, which gives small sunken dark spots, or with frosty pod, which is not known in Africa.


## Damage and symptoms
A brown spot spreads to cover the whole pod, which can turn fully black within about ten days. The beans inside rot. The same fungus can also cause cankers on the trunk, flower cushion damage, and seedling blight. In heavy attacks, most of the crop can be lost in the wet season.


## Conditions that favour it
Wet, humid weather and rain splash. Many pods on the tree. Dense shade and poor airflow. Rotting pods left on trees or on the ground, and mummified pods. Insects and ants can also carry spores.


## Cultural and prevention practices
Remove and bury or destroy diseased pods regularly; in a Cameroon trial, weekly removal was studied for slowing spread. Harvest ripe pods often. Prune and thin the shade and cut weeds so air can move through the farm. Remove mummified pods. Sanitation plus a limited number of fungicide sprays worked better than sanitation alone in trials in Ghana, and sanitation on the trunk helped reduce infection in the canopy.


## Biological and low-risk controls
Researchers are testing lower-impact options such as phosphonate injections and treatments that are less harmful than repeated copper use, but these are not yet general farm practice. Some cocoa varieties are more tolerant; ask about them when replanting.


## Chemical control (registered products only, label rates, protective equipment)
Spray only if scouting shows the problem is spreading. Use only products registered in Nigeria (NAFDAC) for this crop and pest, bought from a licensed dealer in the original labelled container. Follow the label for rate, timing, and the waiting period before harvest. Wear protective equipment (gloves, long sleeves, boots, mask, eye cover), never spray against the wind or near water, and keep children and animals away. Ask the extension officer which products are currently approved. Copper-based fungicides, and fungicides that combine copper with metalaxyl, are the commonly recommended options in West Africa, usually applied several times during the wet season; the number and timing of sprays depends on local advice, so ask CRIN or the extension service. Repeated copper use builds up in soil, so do not exceed what the label allows.


## When to contact an extension officer
Contact an extension officer or CRIN if black pods are spreading despite regular removal, to plan a spray schedule, or if you are unsure whether pods have black pod, mirid damage, or something else. If pods show a thick white to cream or tan powdery growth together with humps on young pods, report it immediately (see the frosty pod entry).
