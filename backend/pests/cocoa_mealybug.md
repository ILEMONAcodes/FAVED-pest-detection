## Identification
Mealybugs are small, soft, oval insects covered with a white, waxy or cottony coating. They gather on young shoots, flower cushions, pods, and branches, and are often tended by ants. Their importance is that they spread cocoa swollen shoot virus disease (CSSVD).


## Damage and symptoms
The insects themselves suck sap and leave white, cottony clusters. The bigger risk is the virus they carry. Signs of swollen shoot disease include swellings on stems and roots, red or yellow banding along the veins of young leaves, small rounded pods, and gradual decline of the tree. Yield loss from severe strains is reported at 15-50%, and trees can die.


## Conditions that favour it
Ants that tend and move mealybugs between trees, wind, and dense canopies where mealybugs move from tree to tree. Planting material from infected farms. Infected trees nearby act as a source of virus.


## Cultural and prevention practices
There is no cure for swollen shoot disease. The recommended practice is to cut out visibly infected trees, and, where programmes recommend it, nearby trees, then replant with tolerant material. Do not move cuttings, pods, or planting material from a farm with swollen shoot symptoms. Scout regularly for white cottony clusters and for symptoms on young leaves. Control ants that tend mealybugs.


## Biological and low-risk controls
Natural enemies such as ladybirds and wasps feed on mealybugs. Barrier planting with other crops around cocoa blocks and shade management have been studied as ways to slow spread. Mild-strain protection of seedlings has been researched but is not a farm practice you can do yourself.


## Chemical control (registered products only, label rates, protective equipment)
Spray only if scouting shows the problem is spreading. Use only products registered in Nigeria (NAFDAC) for this crop and pest, bought from a licensed dealer in the original labelled container. Follow the label for rate, timing, and the waiting period before harvest. Wear protective equipment (gloves, long sleeves, boots, mask, eye cover), never spray against the wind or near water, and keep children and animals away. Ask the extension officer which products are currently approved. Reviews report that insecticides do not work well against mealybugs and that older systemic chemicals harmed beneficial insects and people, so spraying alone will not stop the disease. Do not spray without advice from CRIN or an extension officer.


## When to contact an extension officer
If you see swollen shoots or red vein banding on young cocoa leaves, tell an extension officer or CRIN before you cut anything or move any plants, because cutting-out is usually organised with the authorities and replanting material needs to be tolerant. Also ask for help if mealybugs are heavy on pods.
