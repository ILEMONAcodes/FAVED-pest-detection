## Identification
Mirids (also called capsids) are small sap-sucking bugs. The brown cocoa mirid and a second species, the black or green mirid, are the main insect pests of cocoa in West Africa. Adults are a few millimetres long with long legs and antennae and are often found on pods and young shoots, especially in shaded parts of the tree. Nymphs are smaller, wingless, and lighter in colour.


## Damage and symptoms
Feeding leaves small sunken spots that turn dark on pods and young stems. On twigs, repeated feeding causes dieback and cankers; fungi can enter the wounds and make the damage worse. Pods can become scarred or stunted. Reported yield loss from mirids in Nigeria ranges from around 30% to as high as 70-80%, depending on the study.


## Conditions that favour it
Dense shade and poor pruning, chupons (unwanted shoots) left on the trees, mummified pods hanging on the tree, and unclean farms. Mirid numbers change with the season and rainfall; local advice on timing may differ between regions.


## Cultural and prevention practices
Cultural control has been tested at the Cocoa Research Institute of Nigeria (CRIN): pruning chupons, regular farm cleaning (phytosanitation), removing mummified pods, and managing shade. In a field study these measures reduced mirid infestation levels by about six times compared with untreated plots. Cocoa genotypes also differ in how much damage they suffer, so ask about tolerant planting material when replanting.


## Biological and low-risk controls
Weaver ants and some other ant species prey on mirids; in laboratory tests the weaver ant killed all the mirids exposed to it. Protect these ants where possible. Researchers have also tested water extracts of neem and other plants against mirids; results are mixed and these are not a replacement for the proven practices above.


## Chemical control (registered products only, label rates, protective equipment)
Spray only if scouting shows the problem is spreading. Use only products registered in Nigeria (NAFDAC) for this crop and pest, bought from a licensed dealer in the original labelled container. Follow the label for rate, timing, and the waiting period before harvest. Wear protective equipment (gloves, long sleeves, boots, mask, eye cover), never spray against the wind or near water, and keep children and animals away. Ask the extension officer which products are currently approved. CRIN holds the national mandate to evaluate and recommend insecticides for cocoa in Nigeria; follow its current recommendations on products and timing rather than older spray calendars.


## When to contact an extension officer
Contact an extension officer or CRIN if you see dieback or many damaged pods, if you are unsure whether damage is from mirids or disease, or when planning a spray programme or replanting with tolerant material.
