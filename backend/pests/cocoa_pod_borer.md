## Identification
IMPORTANT: the cocoa pod borer (a small moth) is known as a pest of cocoa in Southeast Asia and the Pacific. I found no evidence that it occurs in West Africa, so a detection in Nigeria is more likely a different problem (for example mirid damage, black pod, another caterpillar, or rodent damage) than the true pod borer. Treat this label as a prompt to look more closely, not a confirmed diagnosis.


## Damage and symptoms
In real pod borer attacks, the tiny caterpillars tunnel into the pod and feed on the pulp around the beans. Pods ripen unevenly or early, the beans stick together, and yield and bean quality drop. Losses in affected regions can be very large.


## Conditions that favour it
Spread happens when infested pods, husks, and other vegetation with pupae are moved. Farms with pods on trees all year give the pest a continuous food supply.


## Cultural and prevention practices
Cultural control is the main approach where the pest is established: harvest all ripe pods often, and remove or bury pod husks and rotting pods. These measures also help against black pod disease and rats. Do not move pods or husks from a suspect farm to other farms.


## Biological and low-risk controls
In affected regions predatory ants kill a good share of the pest, and cultural controls are preferred over pesticides. Parasitoids exist but have not been dependable for control.


## Chemical control (registered products only, label rates, protective equipment)
Do not spray for pod borer on a suspicion. Spray only if scouting shows the problem is spreading. Use only products registered in Nigeria (NAFDAC) for this crop and pest, bought from a licensed dealer in the original labelled container. Follow the label for rate, timing, and the waiting period before harvest. Wear protective equipment (gloves, long sleeves, boots, mask, eye cover), never spray against the wind or near water, and keep children and animals away. Ask the extension officer which products are currently approved.


## When to contact an extension officer
If the pod really shows tunnelling in the pulp with stuck-together beans, do not move the pods. Take clear photos of the pod outside and cut open, and report to the extension office, CRIN, or the Nigeria Agricultural Quarantine Service so the pest can be confirmed. For an ordinary farm problem, ask the extension officer to check for mirid or black pod damage.
